# MoE on a single A100-80GB: model candidates, kernel landscape, megakernel prior art, and fusion-granularity evidence

Research report for a hand-written persistent CUDA megakernel (task-graph, cooperative launch, WMMA) for Qwen3-0.6B decode, integrated into a nano-vLLM fork, targeting **one NVIDIA A100-80GB (108 SMs, sm_80)**.

**How to read the evidence labels used below**

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[measured]** | Numbers reported by the authors from a benchmark on named hardware, with a named baseline. Peer review status varies; noted per item. |
| **[vendor]** | Numbers from a vendor/company engineering blog or product report. Self-reported, methodology not independently reproducible. |
| **[derived]** | My own arithmetic from official config.json / datasheet numbers. Method stated so you can reproduce it. |
| **[unverified]** | I could not confirm this; treat as a lead, not a fact. |

**A note on dates.** Several of the most directly relevant papers (UniEP, MonoMoE, RaMP, Weave, TEMPO, AutoMegaKernel) are from 2026, later than the "2024–2025 preferred" window in the request. I include them because they are the closest prior art to what this project is doing, and I label their dates explicitly.

## 0. The hardware constraint that shapes every decision (sm_80)

Three facts about A100-80GB drive most of the conclusions in this report:

1. **No FP8.** The A100 architecture whitepaper's "A100 Tensor Cores Support All DL Data Types" section and its performance table list TF32, FP16, BF16, INT8, INT4 and binary tensor-core types. FP8 does not appear anywhere in the document. [A100 whitepaper PDF](https://www.nvidia.com/content/dam/en-zz/Solutions/Data-Center/nvidia-ampere-architecture-whitepaper.pdf). A100 has INT8 (624 TOPS dense / 1248 sparse) and INT4 (1248 / 2496 TOPS) tensor cores, and FP16/BF16 at 312 TFLOPS dense each.
2. **Bandwidth.** A100-80GB SXM = **2,039 GB/s**; A100-80GB PCIe = 1,935 GB/s; A100-40GB = 1,555 GB/s. [A100 datasheet PDF](https://www.nvidia.com/content/dam/en-zz/Solutions/Data-Center/a100/pdf/nvidia-a100-datasheet-us-nvidia-1758950-r4-web.pdf). The whitepaper also states GA100 has 128 SMs and that the A100 product has **108 SMs**, matching your card.
3. **The modern megakernel literature is Hopper/Blackwell-only.** Every published MoE (or LLM) megakernel I found targets sm_90/sm_90a (wgmma + TMA) or sm_100/sm_103. Concretely:
   - DeepGEMM's requirements list "NVIDIA SM90 or SM100 architecture GPU", CUDA ≥ 12.9, CUTLASS ≥ 4.0. [DeepGEMM README](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepGEMM)
   - MoK requires "NVIDIA Blackwell SM100 or SM103 GPUs (e.g., GB200 NVL72 or GB300 NVL72)", CUDA ≥ 13.0, torch ≥ 2.10. [MoK README](https://github.com/cursor/mixture-of-kittens)
   - Alpha-MoE "is optimized for the Hopper architecture" and uses WGMMA. [Alpha-MoE report](https://aleph-alpha.com/blog-assets/Alpha-MoE_A-Megakernel-for-faster-Tensor-Parallel-Inference_Report.pdf)
   - Cohere's decode megakernel states: "Our GEMM implementation relies heavily on tensor core instruction, even for batch size 1 because we found that wgmma is slightly faster in our cases compared to CUDA core and reduces register pressure", and its ABI assumes "the standard producer/consumer warp-specialization pattern from a standalone Hopper GEMM" with TMA loads. [Cohere blog](https://cohere.com/blog/megakernels)

   **Implication:** on sm_80 you have `cp.async` (present on A100 per the whitepaper's "Asynchronous Copy" section), `mma.sync` PTX / `nvcuda::wmma`, cooperative groups grid sync, and 164 KB max shared memory per SM. You do **not** have wgmma, TMA, thread-block clusters / distributed shared memory, or programmatic dependent launch. You cannot port any of the above kernels; you can only borrow their *designs*.

**A caveat that should temper the whole project plan.** AutoMegaKernel (arXiv:2606.09682, 2026-06-08) is a system that compiles a HuggingFace Llama-family model into a single persistent cooperative CUDA kernel, validated token-for-token against HF greedy decode, and retargets **sm_80/sm_90/sm_120 from one codebase**. Its own abstract reports: an int8 (W8A16) megakernel beats CUDA-graphed cuBLAS bf16 at batch-1 decode on "inference-class" GPUs (L4 up to 1.33x, L40S 1.25–1.27x, A10G up to 1.08x, RTX 5090 1.19–1.23x) but **"AMK trails cuBLAS on the high-bandwidth training-class A100/H100, where the harness localizes the cross-SM-sync bottleneck; we report the gap plainly."** [arXiv:2606.09682](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09682) **[measured, author-reported, not peer-reviewed, and the comparison is precision-asymmetric: W8A16 megakernel vs bf16 cuBLAS, at decode position 0, largest checkpoint TinyLlama-1.1B]**. This is the single most important data point for your project: a credible full-forward megakernel implementation *loses to CUDA-graphed cuBLAS on A100 specifically*, and attributes the loss to the cost of cross-SM synchronization. Plan for the possibility that your baseline CUDA-graph comparison arm wins on this GPU.

## 1. Small MoE model candidates on one A100-80GB

### 1.1 Method

All "**[derived]**" parameter counts below come from parsing each model's official `config.json` (fetched from the HuggingFace mirror `hf-mirror.com`, which serves the same files as `huggingface.co/<repo>/raw/main/config.json`) and applying the standard transformer parameter arithmetic: embeddings (tied or untied per config), per-layer attention projections, router, and (experts × 3·h·d_expert) FFN terms; for DeepSeek-V2-family, the MLA projections and `first_k_dense_replace` dense layers. Where I quote a **published** figure I mark it **[vendor]** and cite the model card.

Two different "active parameter" conventions are in play and they differ by ~10%:
- **active incl. embeddings** — what most papers mean;
- **per-token streamed bytes** — what actually determines decode speed, which excludes the embedding table, *includes* the full `lm_head` when embeddings are untied (you must read all of it to compute logits), and adds the KV-cache slice.

The second is the number that matters for a decode megakernel, so I compute it separately in §1.3.

### 1.3 The number that actually decides decode speed on one A100

At batch size 1, decode is pure weight-streaming. The bytes you must move per token are: all attention projections + all routers + (top-k/E of expert FFN weights) + shared experts + the full `lm_head` (when untied) + the KV slice. I compute this and divide into 2,039 GB/s for a speed-of-light (SoL) ceiling. **[derived]** — this is my arithmetic, not a measured result, and it assumes perfect bandwidth utilization (no padding waste, no launch gaps, no routing overhead). Real kernels land well below it: Cohere measured vLLM at 39% of an equivalent SoL calculation and their own megakernel at 62% [vendor]. Treat these as ceilings, not predictions.

**Why this matters for the portfolio project.** Qwen3-30B-A3B is *four times larger* than Qwen3-0.6B in total parameters but only **2.8x more expensive per decode token** at batch 1. Meanwhile the *whole-model* weight footprint grows 51x (1.19 → 61.0 GB), which changes the problem from "fit in cache and stream fast" to "you have 61 GB of weights and only touch 6.1 GB of them per token". That is a genuinely different optimization problem, and it is the reason MoE decode is interesting: **the ratio of weight-bytes-in-HBM to weight-bytes-touched-per-token is 10:1**, so cache behaviour and expert layout start to matter. This is also exactly what the 2026 literature is about (§2, §5).

**Practical quantization warning for A100.** FP8 checkpoints on sm_80 get you FP8 *storage* (halved streaming bytes) but not FP8 *math*: there are no FP8 tensor cores, so the kernel must dequantize to BF16/FP16 before the MMA (a W8A16 scheme). There is direct evidence that this pattern is often implemented badly: a 2026 paper on INT8 GEMM for diffusion transformers reports that on consumer Ampere, "the production 'INT8' forward quantizes weights and activations only to immediately dequantize them back to bf16 and run a bf16 matrix multiply, never engaging the GPU's [INT8 tensor cores]" [arXiv:2606.14598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14598) **[measured, author-reported]**. The mature A100-native path is **Marlin** (W4A16/W8A16 for Ampere-class fp16 tensor cores): vLLM's MoE kernel table lists a `marlin` experts kernel supporting `uint4, uint8, fp8, fp4` [vLLM MoE kernel features doc](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/main/docs/design/moe_kernel_features.md). Note that vLLM's `cutlass_fp4`, `flashinfer`, and `trtllm` MoE kernels are NVFP4/MXFP4 and Hopper/Blackwell-oriented; on sm_80 the realistic choices are `triton` and `marlin`.

### 1.5 Recommendation on model choice (my assessment)

- **OLMoE-1B-7B is the best MoE target for a single A100**, if the goal is to make the *MoE megakernel* the contribution rather than the model. Reasons: 13.8 GB bf16 fits with room for a large KV pool; 16 layers only; expert FFN dim 1024 with 64 experts top-8 gives exactly the "few tokens per expert" regime where the 2026 literature says token-major grouped GEMMs waste work (§5); and it is a *small, fully open, well-documented* model (weights, data, code, logs) [[OLMoE paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02060)], which makes a credible baseline story.
- **Qwen3-30B-A3B is the best target if the goal is resume-relevant scale**, because it is the model practitioners actually deploy and it is the MoE architecture every 2025–2026 kernel paper benchmarks (DeepSeek-V3-style: shared expert + many fine-grained routed experts). At 61 GB bf16 it fits but leaves only ~19 GB for KV/workspace; FP8 (30.5 GB) or INT4 (15.3 GB) storage gives much more headroom and makes larger batches viable.
- **DeepSeek-V2-Lite is the most interesting *systems* target** because MLA gives you a 0.25 GB KV slice at 8K vs 0.81 GB for Qwen3-30B-A3B, plus shared-expert routing, plus top-6-of-64. But MLA is a substantial extra implementation burden and the official card targets 40 GB, i.e. it's a "small" model where the megakernel gain may be modest.
- **Granite MoE (1B-A400M / 3B-A800M)** is the most *aggressive* fine-grained case (32–40 experts, expert hidden dim **512**). At that granularity, per-expert tiles are 512×1024, so any 128-aligned grouped-GEMM tile wastes most of its compute. That is a compelling motivation for a weight-major/persistent design, but the models are weak, which weakens the "why should anyone care" narrative.
- **Mixtral-8x7B and Phi-3.5-MoE do not fit in 80 GB bf16** (93.4 GB, 88.8 GB) — only with FP8/INT4 storage. Include at most as a footnote.
- **Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B is not practical** on one A100 (≈160 GB bf16).

## 2. MoE inference optimization as a research and engineering area

### 2.1 The recognized hard problems

**(a) Grouped GEMM vs. a loop over experts.** The naive implementation runs one GEMM per expert per layer. With 128 experts and 48 layers that is up to 6,144 small GEMM launches per token, each with its own grid and its own weight-tile fetch. "Grouped GEMM" batches experts into one kernel by concatenating their token segments along M. DeepGEMM states the constraint precisely: "Unlike traditional grouped GEMMs in CUTLASS, DeepGEMM groups only the M-axis, while N and K must remain fixed. This design is tailored for scenarios where experts in an MoE model share the same shape", and **"each expert segment must be aligned to the GEMM M block size"** [DeepGEMM README](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepGEMM). That alignment requirement is the source of padding waste.

**(b) Expert routing / dispatch overhead.** Permutation, gather/scatter, and unpermutation. Cursor's analysis of the conventional MoE path claims "Five of the eight stages in the traditional path exist purely to manage data layout for the expert-centric view and perform no actual computation" — specifically padding, scatter, and the separate combine/reduction step [Cursor warp decode](https://cursor.com/blog/warp-decode) **[vendor]**.

**(c) Load imbalance, EPLB and expert replication.** DeepSeek open-sourced EPLB: "we adopt a **redundant experts** strategy that duplicates heavy-loaded experts. Then, we heuristically pack the duplicated experts to GPUs to ensure load balancing", using "group-limited expert routing ... to place the experts of the same group to the same node to reduce inter-node data traffic" [deepseek-ai/EPLB README](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/EPLB). vLLM ships an EPLB implementation (`vllm/distributed/eplb/`). The training-side origin is the auxiliary-loss-free balancing strategy [arXiv:2408.15664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15664), used in DeepSeek-V3 [arXiv:2412.19437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437).

**(d) The load-balancing objective is regime-dependent — and the naive one is wrong.** METRO: "existing approaches aim to balance the number of tokens each GPU processes. Surprisingly, we find that this objective **degrades** performance rather than improving it when processing is memory-bound — a common occurrence in MoE serving, especially in the decode phase ... balancing the number of tokens processed per GPU **increases the number of activated experts**, exacerbating memory pressure." Their fix balances *activated experts* instead, and they report **11–22% lower decode latency and 3–21% higher total token throughput** for Qwen3 and DeepSeek-V3 serving, evaluated "on both real systems (**vLLM over 8 A100 GPUs**) and a proprietary simulator (8–16 B200 GPUs)" [arXiv:2512.09277](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.09277) **[measured, preprint; note the A100 result is exactly the GPU class you have]**.

**(e) Expert parallelism, TP, all-to-all, "wide-EP".** In EP the expert dimension is sharded and tokens are dispatched/combined with all-to-all; this is the dominant cost at scale. Occult reports all-to-all "consum[ing] over 40% runtime in large-scale training" [arXiv:2505.13345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13345). **For your project this whole axis is irrelevant** — with one GPU there is no dispatch/combine and no all-to-all. That is worth stating explicitly in a portfolio README, because it removes the single largest cost centre in the MoE systems literature and means single-GPU results are not comparable to multi-GPU results.

**(f) Prefill vs. decode asymmetry.** Prefill has many tokens per expert → grouped GEMM is compute-bound and efficient. Decode at small batch has ~1 token per expert → the kernel is bandwidth-bound and dominated by weight streaming, with poor tile occupancy. Cursor states this cleanly: "This works well for prefill and large batches, where the shared work per expert amortizes the overhead of organizing the data. But during the autoregressive decode step, where we only produce one token at a time, there isn't enough shared work to justify it" [Cursor warp decode](https://cursor.com/blog/warp-decode) **[vendor]**.

**(g) Memory-bound vs. compute-bound regime and the crossover.** TEMPO gives the sharpest quantified statement I found: "below **n\* ≈ 156–168 tokens**, HBM weight streaming dominates — cost attaches to *activated replicas*, not tokens; above it, grouped GEMM rounds tokens to **128-tile M-tiles**, so *splitting* an expert adds padded compute." They model this as a max-affine profile `t = max(a + bG, c + βN)` and report that "realistic decode batches hold hot experts in the linear regime and cold in the flat [regime] **simultaneously**", with proxy dispatches differing by 1.4–1.6x in modelled block time. Their dispatcher stays within 1% of the best fixed baseline everywhere and wins up to 15.5% where regimes mix; end-to-end on Qwen3-235B it gains 4–6% throughput and cuts p99 latency ~15.6% [arXiv:2608.13057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13057) **[measured, preprint, 2026]**.

## 3. Existing megakernel / persistent-kernel work, especially for MoE

### 3.1 UniEP (ByteDance / Tsinghua)

**"UniEP: Unified Expert-Parallel MoE MegaKernel for LLM Training"**, Size Zheng, Xuegui Zheng, Li-wen Chang, Jidong Zhai — **arXiv:2604.19241, 2026-04-21**. [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19241)

- **What is fused:** "UniEP fuses the MoE **communication and computation** into MegaKernels", unifying diverse EP optimization strategies into one abstraction, with "a deterministic token ordering mechanism that guarantees numerical consistency with sequential execution, even under aggressive overlap schedules."
- **Hardware:** "GPU clusters equipped with **NVIDIA Hopper GPUs**" (number of GPUs not stated in the abstract) **[unverified: exact GPU count]**.
- **Reported result:** "**1.03x–1.38x speedups over state-of-the-art work**", framed as mitigating communication bottlenecks while meeting training precision requirements.
- **Training vs. inference:** this is a **training** system. Its central mechanism is *overlapping MoE all-to-all communication with expert computation* — there is no communication on a single GPU, so **the core technique does not transfer**. What does transfer is the *structural* idea: fuse the whole MoE layer into one persistent kernel with a task-graph, and make the schedule deterministic/verifiable.
- Note: I also found an ACM DL entry "UniEP: Unified Expert-Parallel MegaKernel MoE for LLM Training" (DOI 10.1145/3806645.3807818); I did not open it, so I can't say whether it differs from the arXiv version **[unverified]**.

### 3.2 Mixture-of-Kittens (MoK, Cursor)

**"Mixture-of-Kittens (MoK)"** — a fully deterministic MoE **training** megakernel "built from first principles for NVL72s". [repo](https://github.com/cursor/mixture-of-kittens), [blog](https://cursor.com/blog/mixture-of-kittens) (dated Aug 4, 2026)

- **What is fused:** "MoK fuses **all MoE computation and communication** into a single kernel, overlapping compute and inter-GPU networking at configurable granularity, and fully eliminates CPU-GPU synchronization." Supports BF16 and MXFP8; covers forward **and backward**; "powers production training of Composer at Cursor."
- **Hardware:** **NVIDIA Blackwell SM100 or SM103 only** (GB200 NVL72 or GB300 NVL72); CUDA ≥ 13.0, torch ≥ 2.10. Config knobs include `fwd_num_comm_sms` / `bwd_num_comm_sms` (recommended 4–52) — i.e. it *statically reserves SMs for communication*, the same design axis Weave later makes dynamic.
- **Reported results** [vendor, with benchmark code in-repo but not independently reproduced]: vs "the fastest baseline" — up to **2.37x** MXFP8 forward, **1.78x** MXFP8 backward, **1.92x** BF16 forward, **1.58x** BF16 backward. In production across several NVL72 racks: **1.41x end-to-end tokens/s**.
- **Transfer to single-GPU inference decode:** **essentially no.** It is multi-GPU training, and its main lever is compute/communication overlap. The MoK blog itself says the move to GB300 NVL72 "changed the problem in two important ways" — one NVLink domain across 72 GPUs, and slow Grace CPUs forcing elimination of CPU-GPU sync. Neither applies to one A100.
- **However**, the MoK blog contains a useful admission and a pointer: "Over the past year, we wrote our own MXFP8 and NVFP4 training kernels and developed the **'warp decode' approach for MoE inference**. But those techniques optimized only the compute portion of the layer and assumed inter-GPU communication would be handled separately." That inference technique is published separately — see §4.2.

### 3.3 Cohere "North Mini Code" megakernel serving engine — the closest thing to your project

[Cohere blog, Sep 8 2026](https://cohere.com/blog/megakernels) **[vendor, but unusually detailed and specific]**

- **Hardware:** BF16 on a **single H100**; **132 SMs** in their kernel description (H100 SXM has 132).
- **Model:** North Mini Code, "**a 30B model with 3.3B parameters active per token**" — an MoE, i.e. essentially the Qwen3-30B-A3B regime.
- **What they fused:** the **entire decode step** into one persistent kernel, one threadblock per SM. Tasks are 32-int32 descriptors written by the host; dependencies are global-memory counters; controller warp prefetches descriptors into a shared-memory ring; **every opcode — including attention and RMSNorm — uses the same 12-warp / 3-warpgroup (controller, producer, storer + 8 consumer warps) ABI**. MoE drains "claim work from a queue instead of reading the coordinates off the descriptor."
- **Baselines and numbers:** they compute a speed-of-light of ~470 tok/s (6.6 GB weights + ~0.5 GB KV at 8K, over 3.35 TB/s) and report **vLLM at 185 tok/s = 39% of SoL**. Their megakernel reaches **292 tok/s = 62% of SoL, 1.58x faster than vLLM at batch 1**, and "**1.25x–1.41x faster than vLLM end-to-end**" across batch sizes out to 256K context "with no measurable loss of accuracy". Max batch size is **8** (a current implementation limit).
- **Transferability to A100:** **partial and instructive, but not portable as code.** Their GEMM relies on **wgmma** and TMA (sm_90a). What transfers: the host-built task-descriptor + counter-barrier + task-interpreter design; the "one ABI for every op" idea; and the weight-prefetch idea (issue weight-tile loads *before* waiting on the activation barrier). On A100 you'd substitute `cp.async` + `mma.sync`/WMMA and lose the TMA multicast and ping-pong scheduling they explicitly credit for matching cuBLAS/FA3 standalone.

### 3.4 ALPHA-MoE (Aleph Alpha)

[blog](https://aleph-alpha.com/en/blog/alpha-moe-a-megakernel-for-faster-tensor-parallel-inference/) (01/12/2025), [report PDF](https://aleph-alpha.com/blog-assets/Alpha-MoE_A-Megakernel-for-faster-Tensor-Parallel-Inference_Report.pdf), [repo](https://github.com/Aleph-Alpha/Alpha-MoE) **[vendor]**

- **What is fused:** steps 2–6 of an MoE layer — **Up-Proj + Gate GEMM → SwiGLU → activation quantization → Down-Proj GEMM → local combine** — into a single persistent kernel for **FP8 W8A8**. Two named techniques: **SwiGLU weight interleaving** (interleave Up and Gate weights in chunks of 8 so each thread already holds both projections' outputs after the first GEMM stage, matching the WGMMA register layout from the PTX ISA) and **Persistent Down Projection** (at aggressive sharding — "we've observed speed improvements with a `moe_intermediate_size` shard of 256" — the whole Up+Gate result fits in shared memory, so the Down projection runs inside a single block with activations resident in SMEM).
- **Hardware:** **Hopper** (WGMMA, producer/consumer pipelines, multi-stage loads, async stores). **FP8 W8A8 is not a thing on A100.**
- **Target regime:** **tensor-parallel multi-GPU**, not single GPU. Their motivation is that at large TP sizes matrix shards become tiny, "shifting operations from compute-bound to memory-bound". This is the opposite of your regime (single GPU, no sharding).
- **Reported:** standalone kernel 1.20x–1.93x vs SGLang (numbers recovered from the report's figures, §2.2); end-to-end Qwen3-Next-80B and DeepSeek-R1 numbers as in §2.2.
- **Transfer:** the **SwiGLU weight-interleaving trick is hardware-specific** (it depends on WGMMA fragment layout). **Persistent Down Projection is conceptually transferable** to A100: `mma.sync` m16n8k16 fragments also have a fixed thread↔element mapping, and you *can* keep intermediate activations in shared memory (164 KB/SM max on A100) so the down-projection reads SMEM instead of HBM. That is a concrete, implementable idea for your project.

### 3.5 Hazy Research — "Look Ma, No Bubbles!" (May 27, 2025)

[blog](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-05-27-no-bubbles)

- **Hardware/model:** single **H100**, Llama-3.2-1B, bf16, batch size 1, 32-token prompt / 128 generated.
- **What is fused:** the entire Llama-1B forward pass into one megakernel, "eliminating kernel boundaries altogether".
- **Numbers** [measured, author-reported]: **78% of H100 memory bandwidth**; **>1.5x** over vLLM and SGLang. They report the baselines use "at most 50% of available GPU bandwidth" on this workload.
- **Mechanisms they credit, with a measured micro-benchmark you can reuse:** three sources of stall — (i) strict kernel ordering means every SM waits for the slowest threadblock of the previous kernel (their example: 512 threadblocks on 148 SMs leaves SMs idle; note **that example is on B200 at 148 SMs**, whereas your A100 has 108); (ii) launch cost — "for a simple dummy kernel ... on an H100, we find that running on a CUDA stream incurs a launch cost of about **2.1 microseconds**, and with CUDA graphs the launch cost only decreases to around **1.3 microseconds**"; (iii) weight/activation load latency after kernel start. They also report that **Programmatic Dependent Launch (PDL)** "still introduces unnecessary stalls because the PDL synchronization mechanism (`cudaGridDependencySynchronize`) is very coarse" — **and PDL is not available on sm_80 anyway**.
- This is the direct ancestor of both the Cohere engine and, structurally, your project.

## 4. Do single-GPU MoE decode megakernels exist publicly?

**Yes — as of 2026 there are at least three credible public results, and one of them is a single-GPU MoE decode megakernel. None of them is on A100/sm_80.**

### 4.1 MonoMoE — a single-GPU MoE decode megakernel (the closest prior art)

**"MonoMoE: An Efficient Fused Mega-kernel for Quantized MoE Decoding"**, Yu Gong, Kailash Budhathoki, Taeho Kim, Haipeng Li, Ashish Khetan — **arXiv:2609.04244, 2026-08-19**. [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04244). Implementation is open source in FlashInfer: [flashinfer/csrc/fused_moe/monomoe](https://github.com/flashinfer-ai/flashinfer/tree/main/csrc/fused_moe/monomoe).

- **Core idea (novel and worth stealing):** existing grouped/batched GEMMs are **token-major** — they build expert-local token tiles and get parallelism from the token dimension. That is exactly wrong in decode, where few tokens reach each expert: it "incurs tile padding and preprocessing, launches short-lived grids that underutilize memory bandwidth, and exposes quantization, activation, and reduction as separate stages." MonoMoE is instead **weight-major**: it "places the complete decode-step token tile on the fine-grained tensor-core **N** dimension and partitions CTAs over **expert-weight tiles**, eliminating expert-local token materialization and reducing padded arithmetic."
- **What is fused:** "A persistent grid fuses **routing, top-k selection, quantization, both expert projections, activation, and reduction in one launch**; warp specialization and readiness flags overlap auxiliary work with the dominant expert-weight stream."
- **Quantization:** block-wise quantized (FP8 in the evaluation).
- **Hardware:** **NVIDIA H200** (sm_90).
- **Reported numbers** **[measured, author-reported, preprint]**: up to **1.54x** over vLLM's Triton Grouped GEMM for the complete routed-MoE operator; **2.20x–3.84x** faster than a "FlashMoE-FP8 adaptation" across models and batch sizes; **up to 18.7%** reduction in end-to-end time per output token across the evaluated FP8 models, "while preserving task accuracy". Integrated with vLLM.
- **A100 relevance:** the *diagnosis* (token-major grouped GEMM is the wrong layout for decode) and the *fix* (weight-major, CTA-per-expert-weight-tile, N-dimension token tile) are **architecture-independent and directly applicable to sm_80 with WMMA/`mma.sync`**. The FP8 part is not.

### 4.2 Cursor "warp decode" — MoE inference on a single GPU, expert-centric vs output-centric

**"Better MoE model inference with warp decode"**, Less Wright, Federico Cassano, Zhiyuan Zhang — [cursor.com/blog/warp-decode](https://cursor.com/blog/warp-decode) (Apr 6, 2026) **[vendor, but with a precise mechanism and stated numbers]**

- **What it is:** not a full-forward megakernel, but a **two-kernel** reorganization of the MoE compute layer (`moe_gate_up_3d_batched`, `moe_down_3d_batched`). Instead of assigning warps to experts, "we assign each warp to **a single output value (neuron)**". Each warp streams the weight rows it needs directly from memory, "aggregates the totals across all eight routed experts in a **single running total**, and writes one result." The down kernel folds each expert's routing weight into one FP32 accumulator and finishes with a `__shfl_xor_sync` butterfly reduce.
- **What it eliminates:** padding (traditional path "pads each expert's token list to power-of-2, or 128 byte, boundaries"); scatter and combine (the 8 intermediate results "never materialize in memory"); and two intermediate buffers — an activation gather buffer and a per-expert output buffer ("8 × 2048 × 2 bytes = **32 KB per token** in BF16, allocated, written, immediately read once, and discarded").
- **Hardware:** **NVIDIA B200** (148 SMs), small-batch decode, MXFP8 weights.
- **Reported numbers** **[vendor]**: **1.84x throughput improvement**; **3.95 TB/s sustained at B=32 = 58% of a measured 6.8 TB/s peak** for contiguous reads (they attribute the gap to "the memory latency cost of the random access patterns that expert routing creates"); correctness: "minimum cosine similarity > 0.999996, maximum absolute difference 0.001953"; and a claimed quality *improvement* — "outputs **1.4x closer to a full FP32 reference**" because activations stay BF16 with FP32 accumulators instead of round-tripping through MXFP8.
- **The key sentence for §5:** "**Warp decode is not a general replacement for expert-centric execution.** Higher-volume workloads like prefill and large-batch inference still benefit from expert-centric packing because many tokens share the same expert, and the cost of organizing them is amortized over enough real computation to be worthwhile."

### 4.3 Cohere North Mini Code — a single-GPU decode megakernel serving engine *with an MoE*

Covered in §3.3. This is, as far as I can tell, "the first fully fledged serving system built around a decode megakernel" (their claim) and it serves a 30B/3.3B-active MoE on **one H100**: **292 tok/s at batch 1 = 62% of SoL, 1.58x over vLLM**, 1.25–1.41x end-to-end across batch sizes, continuous batching + paged attention + ragged lengths, max batch 8. [Cohere blog](https://cohere.com/blog/megakernels) **[vendor]**

### 4.4 What I could **not** find

- **No public single-GPU MoE decode megakernel targeting A100 / sm_80.** Every result above is Hopper or Blackwell. AutoMegaKernel is the only sm_80-retargetable full-forward megakernel I found, and it explicitly reports losing to CUDA-graphed cuBLAS on A100/H100 [arXiv:2606.09682](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09682).
- **No published head-to-head of a single-GPU *MoE* megakernel vs a CUDA-graph baseline on A100.** MonoMoE's baseline is vLLM's Triton grouped GEMM (not a CUDA graph); Cohere's baseline is vLLM end-to-end (they do not state whether CUDA graphs were enabled); Hazy measures CUDA-graph launch cost (1.3 µs vs 2.1 µs on H100) but benchmarks against non-graphed vLLM/SGLang. **So the specific comparison your project proposes — MoE decode megakernel vs CUDA-graphed cuBLAS/grouped-GEMM baseline on one A100 — appears to be genuinely unoccupied territory.** That is good news for novelty and bad news for predictability.

## 5. Fusion granularity vs. performance: evidence that partial fusion can beat full fusion

### 5.1 The 2013 cautionary tale is real and quantified

**Laine, Karras, Aila, "Megakernels Considered Harmful: Wavefront Path Tracing on GPUs", High-Performance Graphics 2013.** [paper PDF](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/publications/laine2013hpg_paper.pdf) · [NVIDIA page](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2013-07_megakernels-considered-harmful-wavefront-path-tracing-gpus) · DOI [10.1145/2492045.2492060](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2492045.2492060)

The mechanism: "Due to SIMT execution model on GPUs, **divergence in control flow carries substantial performance penalties, as does high register usage that lessens the latency-hiding capability** that is essential for the high-latency, high-bandwidth memory system of a GPU."

The measured result (their Table 1, millions of completed paths per second):

| Scene | tris | Megakernel | Wavefront | Speedup for **splitting** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CARPAINT | 9.5K | 42.99 | 58.38 | **+36%** |
| CITY | 879K | 5.41 | 9.70 | **+79%** |
| CONFERENCE | 283K | 2.71 | 8.71 | **+221%** |

Their Nsight measurement for the CARPAINT scene: "the thread utilization of the megakernel is only **23%**, whereas the wavefront variant has **53%** overall thread utilization." And their conclusion is a direct answer to your design question: "**While there are overheads associated with storing path data in memory between kernel launches, management of the queues, and launching the kernels, these are well outweighed by the benefits.**"

Two caveats before you cite this: it is ray tracing, not LLM inference; and its megakernel is *not* a persistent/task-graph megakernel — it is a single large divergent kernel with no fine-grained sync. Its lesson is therefore specifically "**do not fuse divergent, register-hungry work into one kernel**", not "persistent kernels are bad". Notably, the wavefront design it advocates is *itself* a queue-based multi-kernel task graph — the same shape as a modern decode megakernel, just with the queue drains split across kernel launches.

### 5.2 Modern LLM-specific evidence on granularity

**(a) Cohere measured that *schedule* alone moves throughput by ~20%, and that simpler fusion-adjacent heuristics can beat sophisticated ones.**

Their wave-order ablation, two 8K-input, uniform-routing runs that "change only wave order" [vendor, from a table in the blog]:

| Batch size | Tuned order (tok/s) | Interleaved | Loss | Attention-first | Loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **291** | 282 | 3% | 236 | **19%** |
| 2 | 423 | 406 | 4% | 364 | 14% |
| 4 | 553 | 531 | 4% | 509 | 8% |
| 8 | 687 | 677 | 1% | 642 | 7% |

A 19% swing at batch 1 from task ordering alone. Two further findings from the same post that cut against "more sophistication is better":
- "We also tried **dependency-affinity placement**: put a task on the same SM as a producer it depends on, hoping to reuse cache and reduce cross-SM handoffs. **It slowed the kernel by 1–2%**, so the released version uses naive round-robin placement."
- "Early in the project, while targeting a dense model, we built several more sophisticated schedulers" — a greedy topology-aware cost-model scheduler and a brute-force search over hundreds of random schedules — "Both delivered about **10% more throughput than plain round-robin on that workload**. **MoE changed the problem**: routing makes the amount and placement of its work dynamic, so those dense-model schedules did not transfer cleanly."
- And on where the gain actually comes from, they rank four mechanisms and say the biggest three are **wave-quantization reduction, false-dependency removal, and weight prefetch** — i.e. the benefits of fusion here come from *finer-grained scheduling*, not merely from collapsing kernel boundaries.

**(b) Cursor's warp decode is an explicit, measured statement that the right granularity flips with the regime.** Output-centric warps win small-batch decode (**1.84x**, and they say it is "not a general replacement"); expert-centric packing wins prefill and large batch. This is the clearest "partial/different fusion granularity beats the alternative" result in the LLM space [vendor].

**(c) TEMPO quantifies the crossover.** n\* ≈ **156–168 tokens** per expert: below it, HBM weight streaming dominates and cost attaches to *activated replicas* rather than tokens; above it, grouped GEMM rounds tokens into 128-wide M tiles and splitting an expert adds padded compute. A max-affine model `t = max(a + bG, c + βN)` captures both; realistic decode batches have some experts in each regime at once [arXiv:2608.13057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13057).

**(d) METRO shows the "obvious" global objective is wrong in the memory-bound regime.** Balancing tokens per GPU *increases* the number of activated experts and makes things worse; balancing activated experts gives **11–22% lower decode latency** on vLLM over 8×A100 [arXiv:2512.09277](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.09277).

**(e) MonoMoE and SonicMoE both attack padding/quantization waste rather than fusion extent.** MonoMoE: token-major layout "incurs tile padding and preprocessing" → weight-major persistent kernel [arXiv:2609.04244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04244). SonicMoE: "sparser MoEs suffer from wasted computations due to padding in Grouped GEMM kernels" → token rounding, **1.16x** extra kernel speedup at high sparsity [arXiv:2512.14080](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14080).

**(f) RaMP: the optimum is not a single point in configuration space.** "The optimal kernel configuration for MoE inference depends on **both batch size and the expert routing distribution**, yet production systems dispatch from batch size alone, leaving **10–70% of kernel throughput unrealized**." Their fix is runtime *polymorphism* across 134–268 configurations, giving 1.22x over static dispatch [arXiv:2604.26039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26039).

**(g) AutoMegaKernel is the direct cautionary data point for your GPU.** A full-forward persistent megakernel that retargets sm_80 *loses to CUDA-graphed cuBLAS bf16 on A100/H100* while winning on L4/L40S/A10G/RTX 5090, with the authors attributing the loss to the cross-SM synchronization bottleneck [arXiv:2606.09682](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09682).

**(h) The strongest *pro*-full-fusion evidence remains Hazy on H100 dense 1B:** 78% of peak bandwidth and >1.5x over vLLM/SGLang, with the specific mechanisms being straggler elimination, ~2.1 µs → ~1.3 µs launch cost, and fine-grained dependency barriers vs PDL's coarse `cudaGridDependencySynchronize` [Hazy blog](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-05-27-no-bubbles) **[measured, author-reported]**.

### 5.3 Synthesis: when full fusion wins, when partial wins

| Full fusion (one kernel, whole step) wins when | Partial / segmented fusion wins when |
|---|---|
| Launch + teardown overhead is a large fraction of the step (small model, small batch, many ops) — Hazy's ~2.1 µs/launch, Ada-MK's 14.6% | The workload is large enough that per-op kernels already amortize launch cost (big batch, prefill, long K) |
| **Wave quantization** dominates: tile counts are not multiples of 108 SMs and every kernel boundary rounds up — Cohere's mechanism #1 | Tile counts *are* well-matched to SM count, so there is nothing to round up |
| Tasks have **varied register/SMEM pressure**, and one kernel must reserve the max, destroying occupancy — Laine's core argument | Fused ops have similar resource needs, so the max is not much worse than the average |
| Dependencies are **fine-grained and asymmetric** (a KV group, one expert's up-projection) so a full-grid barrier is a false dependency — Cohere's mechanism #2 | Dependencies are genuinely all-to-all between stages |
| **Weight prefetch across barriers** is possible because weights are immutable — Cohere's mechanism #3 | Working set exceeds L2, so prefetch just evicts what you need |
| Control flow is **uniform** (dense transformer, fixed op sequence) | Control flow is **data-dependent and divergent** (routing! variable path lengths! variable sequence lengths) — Laine's exact failure mode, and RaMP's finding that MoE optimum depends on the live routing histogram |
| The op sequence is **static** so a host-built schedule can be reused across steps | Task counts change every step (continuous batching, ragged lengths) — Cohere's answer was *hybrid*: static schedule + local work-stealing claimers for exactly the attention and MoE drains |
| **No cross-SM sync is needed at all** (e.g. tensor-parallel-free single GPU with a simple dataflow) | Cross-SM synchronization itself is the bottleneck — AutoMegaKernel's A100/H100 failure mode |

**The MoE-specific twist:** MoE decode adds *data-dependent divergence and dynamic work volume* to a workload whose appeal was uniformity. That is precisely the combination Laine 2013 says a megakernel handles badly. The 2026 literature's responses are (i) keep the persistent kernel but make scheduling dynamic (Weave, RaMP, MonoMoE's work queues/readiness flags), (ii) keep the fine-grained barriers but add work-stealing queues for the dynamic stages (Cohere), or (iii) drop the persistent kernel for the MoE layer specifically and reorganize around outputs (Cursor warp decode). **All three are arguments for "persistent task-graph, but not naively one-kernel-fuses-everything", which is exactly the design space your project sits in.**

## 6. Concrete implications for your project

1. **Your baseline must be CUDA-graphed, not just eager.** Hazy measured 2.1 µs/launch on a stream vs 1.3 µs with CUDA graphs on H100; Ada-MK reports launch overhead alone at 14.6% of end-to-end. If you compare against non-graphed vLLM you will overstate your win. Note that no public single-GPU-MoE-megakernel paper I found states whether its baseline used graphs.
2. **Expect A100 to be the hard case, and say so.** AutoMegaKernel — the only sm_80-retargetable full-forward megakernel I found — reports losing to CUDA-graphed cuBLAS on A100/H100 due to cross-SM sync [arXiv:2606.09682](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09682). A report that says "our megakernel is 1.1x over CUDA-graphed cuBLAS on A100 at batch 1, and here's the Nsight breakdown of where the sync cost goes" is a *stronger* portfolio artifact than a claimed 2x that a reviewer will not believe. Negative/qualified results with a mechanism analysis are credible and interviewable.
3. **If you add MoE, pick OLMoE-1B-7B as the primary target and Qwen3-30B-A3B as the stretch target.** OLMoE: 13.8 GB bf16, 16 layers, 64 experts top-8, expert dim 1024, per-token stream 2.25 GB → 905 tok/s SoL ceiling. Qwen3-30B-A3B: 61 GB bf16 (or 30.5 GB FP8 storage / 15.3 GB INT4), 48 layers, 128 experts top-8, per-token stream 3.85 GB → 530 tok/s SoL. Both are supported by vLLM and SGLang, so you always have a credible comparison arm.
4. **The most defensible MoE-specific contribution on sm_80 is the weight-major layout**, i.e. MonoMoE's insight: put the decode-step token tile on the tensor-core N dimension and partition CTAs over expert-weight tiles, instead of building per-expert token tiles along M with 128-alignment padding. It needs no FP8, so it maps onto WMMA/`mma.sync` + `cp.async`. Combined with Cursor's stage/buffer-elimination list (no padding, no gather buffer, no per-expert output buffer, no separate combine) that is a coherent, citable design story.
5. **Consider RaMP's polymorphism idea as your "partial fusion" arm.** Instead of "full fusion vs one-kernel-per-op", implement 2–4 megakernel variants (or one kernel with a compile-time-selected schedule) and dispatch on the runtime expert histogram. RaMP reports 10–70% of kernel throughput lost by batch-size-only dispatch, 1.22x from polymorphic selection, and 0.93% mean regret vs exhaustive search [arXiv:2604.26039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26039). That gives you a *third* arm that is a genuine research-flavoured contribution rather than just an engineering A/B.
6. **Be explicit that single-GPU removes the dominant cost centre.** Every MoE systems paper's biggest lever is dispatch/combine all-to-all (Occult: >40% of runtime in large-scale training [arXiv:2505.13345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13345); MoK, UniEP, Weave, Perseus are all about overlapping it). On one A100 that cost is exactly zero, so your results are not comparable to theirs, and your ceiling is bandwidth, not interconnect. Stating this shows you know the field.
7. **Quantization on A100: use W4A16/W8A16 (Marlin-style), not FP8.** A100 has INT8/INT4 tensor cores but no FP8 tensor cores [A100 whitepaper](https://www.nvidia.com/content/dam/en-zz/Solutions/Data-Center/nvidia-ampere-architecture-whitepaper.pdf); FP8 checkpoints buy you storage only. vLLM's `marlin` MoE experts path is the sm_80-native option [vLLM MoE kernel features](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/main/docs/design/moe_kernel_features.md). Also see the 2026 report that "INT8" forwards on Ampere frequently dequantize to bf16 and never engage INT8 tensor cores [arXiv:2606.14598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14598).
8. **Do not plan to port UniEP/MoK/Alpha-MoE/Cohere kernels.** Hard requirements are sm_90a (wgmma/TMA) or sm_100/103, and DeepGEMM requires SM90/SM100. Borrow the designs — descriptor + counter barriers + one ABI per op (Cohere), SM-level task graph (MPK), work-stealing drains for dynamic stages (Cohere), weight-major layout (MonoMoE), persistent down-projection in SMEM (Alpha-MoE) — and re-implement on `cp.async` + WMMA.
